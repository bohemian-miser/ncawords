Dynamic Kernel NCA — Organism Comparison & Physics Interpolation

Two organisms, one shared 16-channel world. Each cell computes its own spatial filters on the fly and updates locally. A painted control field smoothly interpolates between both physics rules cell by cell.

The per-cell update rule

Every cell carries a state vector of 16 numbers. The first three are RGB, the fourth is an “aliveness” alpha, and the remaining twelve are hidden chemical channels. There are no global coordinates; every cell runs the exact same ~8,000-parameter rule in parallel.

An update step proceeds in three stages. First, perception: each channel is filtered with the cell’s current state and two dynamic spatial convolutions, yielding a 48-number summary of the cell and its surroundings. That vector passes through a two-layer pointwise MLP (48 → 128 → 16, ReLU) whose final layer is zero-initialized so an untrained network changes nothing. The output is a proposed velocity Δx.

Cells update asynchronously: each cell fires with probability 0.5 per step, adding its proposed Δx to its state. This stochastic clock prevents artificial synchronization across the grid.

Dynamic perception: learning the lens

Classic NCAs use fixed Sobel filters for perception. That forces every cell — whether on the outer membrane, deep in the interior, or in empty vacuum — to inspect its neighborhood through the exact same spatial lens.

Here, perception is dynamic. Each cell looks at its immediate 3×3 neighborhood to synthesize its own 5×5 filter. A tiny gating calculation produces four mixture weights that blend four fixed basis primitives: an edge filter, a Laplacian curvature filter, a Gaussian ring, and a center-surround operator.

Cells along organism borders dial up the Laplacian to sharpen boundaries; cells deep inside dial up rings and center-surround filters to stabilize bulk mass. You can see this live by hovering over the canvas above: crossing a boundary immediately shifts the mixture meters.

Blending two creatures: the interpolation trick

A neural network’s weights are non-linear: averaging the weights of two trained models destroys their attractors and kills both organisms. But because both models operate on the same 16-channel physics, their update deltas live in the same coordinate space.

At each step, we query Model A for ΔxA and Model B for ΔxB, then apply a local linear blend weighted by the painted control field: 

Δx = (1 − B) · ΔxA + B · ΔxB
x ← x + fire · Δx

Where B = 0, Model A rules. Where B = 1, Model B rules. Along the seam, the two physics rules negotiate a stable interface without parameter collapse.

Is it a mask? The alpha viability rule

In code, the original Mordvintsev et al. rule is often written as alive_mask = (max_pool(alpha, 3) > 0.1). Calling it a “mask” makes it sound like an external stencil stamped onto the simulation from above.

It is not an external mask. It is an intrinsic cellular viability threshold operating directly on the cell’s own state. Channel 3 (α) represents cell membrane density. If a cell and all eight of its immediate neighbors have α ≤ 0.1, the cell has lost contact with living tissue and undergoes apoptosis — its state resets to zero. For morphogenesis from a single seed, this rule is biologically natural: it prevents drifting debris in empty space.

The toggle in the toolbar lets you turn this apoptosis rule off. When disabled, you can test whether noise-trained models can hold their own boundaries and keep the vacuum silent purely through their learned internal weights.
